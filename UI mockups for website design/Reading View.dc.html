CASE STUDY · AIRBNB, 2008

The bed that cost nothing to make

ESTIMATED READING TIME 12 MIN · DAY 17 · PRICING POWER

Three founders in San Francisco had a loft, two air mattresses and a design conference arriving in a city with no hotel rooms left. The first listing went up at eighty dollars a night. It was not a considered number — it was a guess, and it sold out.

What made the guess interesting was the cost behind it. Adding one guest to a room you already heat costs almost nothing — a set of sheets, some hot water, an hour of your evening. In the language of the chapter, marginal cost was near zero.

A hotel cannot say that. Its cost per room carries the building, the front desk, the cleaning contract and the loan. So a hotel prices from cost upward, and a host with a spare room can price from value downward — starting from what the night is worth to someone who has nowhere else to sleep.

When the cost of one more customer approaches zero, price stops being a fact about production and becomes a question about the buyer.

This is why the company's own pricing tools eventually stopped asking hosts what their room cost them and started showing what similar rooms sold for that weekend. The signal moved from the supply side to the demand side, and the platform's job became reading it faster than any individual host could.

There is a limit, and it is worth naming before the quiz. Willingness to pay is not infinite patience: price above what the guest believes the night is worth and the booking does not happen at all. Near-zero marginal cost widens the range you can charge inside. It does not remove the ceiling.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
1

Marginal cost near zero widens the price range, it does not remove the ceiling.

2

Hotels price from cost upward; hosts price from value downward.

3

Scarcity in the city, not generosity in the host, set the eighty-dollar night.
